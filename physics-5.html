TOPIC 01

Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics describes how energy is transferred and transformed in systems involving particles, temperature, pressure and volume.

Internal Energy

The internal energy of a system is the sum of the random kinetic energy and intermolecular potential energy of all its particles.

Key definition:

Internal energy is the total microscopic energy associated with the particles in a system.

Temperature and Particle Motion

Temperature is related to the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. Increasing the temperature increases the average random kinetic energy of the particles.

Specific Heat Capacity

The specific heat capacity of a substance is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of the substance by 1 K without a change of state.

ΔE = mcΔθ

Specific Latent Heat

Specific latent heat is the energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without changing its temperature.

E = ml

Changes of State

  • Melting changes a solid into a liquid.
  • Boiling changes a liquid into a gas.
  • During a change of state, supplied energy changes intermolecular potential energy rather than temperature.

Ideal Gas Equation

An ideal gas is modelled as particles whose volume is negligible compared with the volume of the gas and whose intermolecular forces are negligible except during collisions.

pV = nRT

Here p is pressure, V is volume, n is amount of substance, R is the molar gas constant and T is absolute temperature.

Absolute Temperature

Gas equations require temperature to be measured in kelvin.

T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15

Pressure of a Gas

Gas pressure results from collisions between particles and the walls of the container. Increasing the average particle speed generally increases the pressure if volume is kept constant.

EXAM TIP

Always check that temperature is in kelvin when using gas-law equations. A Celsius temperature cannot be used directly in pV = nRT.

TOPIC 02

Nuclear Decay

Radioactive decay is a spontaneous and random process in which an unstable nucleus changes into another nucleus.

Activity

Activity is the number of nuclear decays occurring per second.

A = λN

Activity is measured in becquerels (Bq).

Decay Constant

The decay constant λ represents the probability per unit time that an individual nucleus will decay.

Exponential Decay

N = N₀e−λt

Activity also follows exponential decay:

A = A₀e−λt

Half-Life

Half-life is the time required for the number of undecayed nuclei, or the activity, to decrease to half its initial value.

T½ = ln(2) / λ

Types of Nuclear Radiation

  • Alpha: helium nuclei consisting of two protons and two neutrons.
  • Beta-minus: electrons emitted from the nucleus when a neutron changes into a proton.
  • Gamma: electromagnetic radiation emitted when a nucleus loses excess energy.

Random Nature of Radioactive Decay

The exact time at which an individual nucleus decays cannot be predicted. However, the behaviour of a large collection of nuclei can be predicted statistically.

Important:

Half-life is independent of the number of undecayed nuclei present. Each nucleus has the same probability of decaying per unit time.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A radioactive isotope has a decay constant of 0.20 s⁻¹. Calculate its half-life.

T½ = ln(2) / λ

T½ = 0.693 / 0.20

T½ = 3.47 s

TOPIC 03

Oscillations and Simple Harmonic Motion

An oscillation is repeated motion about an equilibrium position. Simple harmonic motion is a special type of oscillation.

Simple Harmonic Motion

An object undergoes simple harmonic motion when its acceleration is proportional to its displacement from equilibrium and directed towards the equilibrium position.

a = −ω²x

Angular Frequency

ω = 2πf = 2π/T

Displacement

A sinusoidal function can be used to describe displacement during SHM.

x = A cos(ωt)

Velocity

v = −Aω sin(ωt)

Maximum Speed

vmax = Aω

Maximum Acceleration

amax = Aω²

Velocity-Displacement Relationship

v² = ω²(A² − x²)

Energy in SHM

In ideal SHM, energy continually transfers between kinetic and potential energy while total mechanical energy remains constant.

E = ½mω²A²
At equilibrium:

Displacement is zero, speed is maximum and acceleration is zero.

At maximum displacement:

Speed is zero, acceleration has maximum magnitude and potential energy is maximum.

TOPIC 04

Springs and Oscillating Systems

A mass attached to a spring can oscillate about its equilibrium position when displaced and released.

Hooke's Law

F = kx

Hooke's law applies while the extension or compression remains within the limit of proportionality.

Elastic Potential Energy

E = ½kx²

Spring-Mass Oscillator

The period of a mass-spring system depends on the mass attached to the spring and the spring constant.

T = 2π√(m/k)

WORKED EXAMPLE

A spring has a spring constant of 200 N m⁻¹ and is stretched by 0.10 m. Calculate the elastic potential energy.

E = ½kx²

E = ½ × 200 × (0.10)²

E = 1.0 J

TOPIC 05

Simple Pendulum

A simple pendulum can approximate simple harmonic motion when the angular displacement is small.

Period of a Pendulum

T = 2π√(l/g)

Factors Affecting the Period

  • Increasing the length increases the period.
  • Increasing gravitational field strength decreases the period.
  • For small oscillations, the period is approximately independent of the amplitude.

Experimental Investigation

A common method is to measure the time for several complete oscillations and calculate the period.

Repeating measurements and using a larger number of oscillations reduces the effect of reaction-time uncertainty.

T² = (4π²/g)l

Therefore a graph of T² against l should be approximately linear, with gradient 4π²/g.

TOPIC 06

Astrophysics

Astrophysics applies physical principles to stars, planets, galaxies and other astronomical systems.

Gravitational Field Strength

g = GM/r²

Gravitational Potential

Vg = −GM/r

Orbital Motion

An object in a circular orbit remains in orbit because the gravitational attraction provides the required centripetal force.

Orbital Speed

v = √(GM/r)

Orbital Period

For objects orbiting the same central mass:

T² ∝ r³
Key idea:

A satellite does not require a continuous forward force to remain in orbit. Gravity continuously changes its direction of motion and provides the centripetal force.

TOPIC 07

Stars and Stellar Evolution

Stars form and evolve as the balance between gravitational forces, pressure and nuclear processes changes over time.

Formation of Stars

Stars form from clouds of gas and dust. Gravity causes the cloud to collapse, increasing density and temperature.

Main Sequence

During the main-sequence stage, stars release energy primarily through nuclear fusion in their cores.

Low and Medium Mass Stars

After leaving the main sequence, a lower-mass star can expand into a red giant before eventually leaving behind a white dwarf.

Massive Stars

Massive stars can become red supergiants and undergo supernova explosions. Their remnants can form neutron stars or black holes.

Remember:

Initial stellar mass is one of the most important factors determining the later evolution of a star.

TOPIC 08

Luminosity and Stellar Measurements

Luminosity

Luminosity is the total power emitted by a star.

L = 4πR²σT⁴

Stefan-Boltzmann Law

The Stefan-Boltzmann law relates the luminosity of a star to its radius and absolute surface temperature.

Intensity

I = L / (4πd²)

The intensity received from a source decreases according to the inverse-square law.

Wien's Displacement Law

λmaxT = constant

A hotter star has its peak wavelength at a shorter wavelength.

L ∝ R²T⁴
Stefan-Boltzmann relationship
I ∝ 1/d²
Inverse-square relationship

TOPIC 09

Stellar Spectra

Spectroscopy allows scientists to obtain information about the composition, temperature and motion of astronomical objects.

Continuous Spectra

A hot, dense object can produce a continuous spectrum containing a broad range of wavelengths.

Emission Spectra

Excited atoms can emit photons at specific wavelengths, producing bright spectral lines.

Absorption Spectra

A cooler gas in front of a continuous source can absorb particular wavelengths, producing dark absorption lines.

Doppler Effect

The observed wavelength of light changes when the source moves relative to the observer.

Δλ / λ = v / c

Redshift

If spectral lines are shifted towards longer wavelengths, the light is redshifted. For relatively small velocities:

z = Δλ / λ ≈ v/c
EXAM TIP

Compare the observed spectral-line wavelength with its known laboratory wavelength. A shift towards longer wavelengths indicates redshift; a shift towards shorter wavelengths indicates blueshift.

TOPIC 10

Cosmology

Cosmology investigates the origin, structure and evolution of the Universe.

Expansion of the Universe

Observations of distant galaxies show that the Universe is expanding.

Hubble's Law

v = H₀d

The recession velocity of a galaxy is approximately proportional to its distance from Earth.

Hubble Constant

H₀ represents the current rate of expansion of the Universe. Its numerical value depends on the units used for distance and velocity.

Big Bang Model

The Big Bang model describes a Universe that began in an extremely hot and dense state and has expanded and cooled over time.

Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation

Cosmic microwave background radiation is electromagnetic radiation originating from the early Universe. Its existence provides important evidence supporting the Big Bang model.

Redshift and Expansion

Light from many distant galaxies is observed to be redshifted. This provides evidence that those galaxies are generally receding from us.

z = Δλ / λ
Key conclusion:

The observation that more distant galaxies generally have greater recession velocities is consistent with an expanding Universe.

TOPIC 11

Critical Density and the Universe

The large-scale evolution of the Universe depends on the balance between its expansion and gravitational effects.

Critical Density

Critical density is the density associated with a particular boundary between different possible large-scale expansion behaviours.

Dark Matter

Dark matter is proposed to account for gravitational effects that cannot be explained by visible matter alone.

Dark Energy

Dark energy is associated with the observed accelerated expansion of the Universe.

Important distinction:

Dark matter is associated primarily with additional gravitational effects, whereas dark energy is associated with the accelerated expansion of the Universe.

PRACTICAL SKILLS

Unit 5 Practical Skills

Practical questions can test planning, measurements, processing data, uncertainties, graphs and evaluation of experimental methods.

Gas Investigation

Investigate relationships between pressure, volume and temperature for a fixed mass of gas.

Radioactive Decay

Measurements of count rate over time can be used to investigate radioactive decay and estimate half-life.

Simple Pendulum

Measure the period for different pendulum lengths and use appropriate graphical analysis to determine gravitational field strength.

Oscillating Spring

Measure the period of oscillation for different masses and investigate the relationship between T² and mass.

Experimental Evaluation

  • Identify independent and dependent variables.
  • Control relevant variables.
  • Repeat measurements.
  • Calculate mean values where appropriate.
  • Identify anomalous results.
  • Consider random and systematic uncertainty.
  • Use suitable graphs.
  • Suggest realistic improvements.
PRACTICAL EXAM TIP

When suggesting an improvement, explain how it would specifically reduce uncertainty or improve the reliability of the result rather than simply saying "repeat the experiment."

EXAM PRACTICE

Unit 5 Mixed Practice

1. A 2.0 kg block is heated through 15 K. Its specific heat capacity is 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Calculate the energy transferred.
Show answer

ΔE = mcΔθ

ΔE = 2.0 × 900 × 15

ΔE = 27 000 J

2. State the meaning of specific latent heat.
Show answer

The specific latent heat is the energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without changing its temperature.

3. A gas has a volume of 0.020 m³ at a pressure of 1.5 × 10⁵ Pa. Calculate the product pV.
Show answer

pV = (1.5 × 10⁵)(0.020)

pV = 3.0 × 10³ Pa m³

4. A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 8.0 days. What fraction remains after 24 days?
Show answer

24 days represents three half-lives.

Initial → 1/2 → 1/4 → 1/8

Fraction remaining = 1/8

5. A radioactive isotope has a decay constant of 0.10 s⁻¹. Calculate its half-life.
Show answer

T½ = ln(2) / λ

T½ = 0.693 / 0.10

T½ = 6.93 s

6. State the condition required for simple harmonic motion.
Show answer

Acceleration must be proportional to displacement from equilibrium and directed towards the equilibrium position.

7. An oscillator has amplitude 0.040 m and angular frequency 12 rad s⁻¹. Calculate its maximum speed.
Show answer

vmax = Aω

vmax = 0.040 × 12

vmax = 0.48 m s⁻¹

8. A spring has k = 150 N m⁻¹ and extension 0.080 m. Calculate its elastic potential energy.
Show answer

E = ½kx²

E = ½ × 150 × (0.080)²

E = 0.48 J

9. State the equation for the period of a simple pendulum.
Show answer

T = 2π√(l/g)

10. What force provides the centripetal force for a planet orbiting a star?
Show answer

The gravitational attraction between the planet and the star provides the centripetal force.

11. A star has luminosity L and is observed from a distance d. State the equation relating intensity to luminosity and distance.
Show answer

I = L / (4πd²)

12. A star's surface temperature increases. What happens to the wavelength at which its emission is maximum?
Show answer

According to Wien's law, increasing temperature causes the peak wavelength to decrease.

Therefore the peak shifts towards shorter wavelengths.

13. State Hubble's law.
Show answer

The recession velocity of a distant galaxy is approximately proportional to its distance from Earth:

v = H₀d

14. Explain how redshift provides evidence that the Universe is expanding.
Show answer

Spectral lines from distant galaxies are observed to be shifted towards longer wavelengths. This indicates that the galaxies are generally moving away from us. The recession velocity increases approximately with distance, providing evidence for an expanding Universe.

15. Explain why the cosmic microwave background radiation provides evidence supporting the Big Bang model.
Show answer

The cosmic microwave background is radiation originating from the early Universe. Its near-uniform presence across the Universe is consistent with the prediction that the early Universe was extremely hot and dense and has expanded and cooled over time.
