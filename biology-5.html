02 — Topic 7

Cellular Respiration

Respiration transfers chemical energy from organic molecules into ATP, which can then power cellular processes.

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O

Energy is released and transferred to ATP.

Stages of aerobic respiration

1 Glycolysis
Cytoplasm
→
2 Link reaction
Matrix
→
3 Krebs cycle
Matrix
→
4 Oxidative phosphorylation
Inner membrane

Glycolysis

Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm and involves the phosphorylation and subsequent splitting of glucose.

  • Glucose is phosphorylated.
  • A six-carbon intermediate is split into two three-carbon molecules.
  • The three-carbon molecules are oxidised.
  • Pyruvate is produced.
  • Reduced NAD is produced.
  • There is a net gain of ATP.
Net ATP gain from glycolysis = 2 ATP

Reduced NAD = 2 molecules per glucose

Link reaction

Pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix and is converted into acetyl coenzyme A.

  1. Pyruvate is decarboxylated.
  2. Hydrogen is removed.
  3. NAD is reduced.
  4. The remaining two-carbon acetyl group combines with coenzyme A.
Pyruvate → Acetyl CoA + CO₂ + reduced NAD

Krebs cycle

Acetyl CoA enters the Krebs cycle. The cycle produces carbon dioxide, reduced coenzymes and ATP.

CO₂

Released during decarboxylation.

Reduced NAD / FAD

Carry hydrogen/electrons to the electron transport chain.

ATP

Produced by substrate-level phosphorylation.

Oxidative phosphorylation

Reduced NAD and reduced FAD donate electrons to electron carriers in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

  1. Electrons pass along the electron transport chain.
  2. Energy released pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane.
  3. A proton concentration gradient forms.
  4. Protons flow through ATP synthase.
  5. ATP is produced by chemiosmosis.
  6. Oxygen acts as the final electron acceptor.
  7. Water is formed.
Oxidative phosphorylation: ATP production associated with the oxidation of reduced coenzymes through the electron transport chain.
03 — Anaerobic Respiration

Anaerobic Respiration

When oxygen availability is insufficient, cells cannot maintain aerobic respiration at the required rate.

In animal cells

Glucose → Lactate + small ATP yield

Lactate can accumulate during intense exercise and is later transported and metabolised when oxygen availability increases.

In yeast

Glucose → Ethanol + CO₂ + ATP
Anaerobic respiration produces much less ATP per glucose molecule than aerobic respiration.

Respiratory quotient

RQ = CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ consumed

Sliding filament mechanism

Muscle contraction occurs when actin filaments slide over myosin filaments, shortening the sarcomere.

  1. A motor neurone stimulates the muscle fibre.
  2. Calcium ions are released.
  3. Calcium binds to troponin.
  4. Tropomyosin moves away from actin's binding sites.
  5. Myosin heads attach to actin.
  6. Myosin heads pivot, pulling actin.
  7. ATP allows the myosin head to detach and reset.
ATP is required for:

• detachment of myosin heads
• re-cocking of myosin heads
• active transport of calcium ions

Antagonistic muscle pairs

Flexor

Contracts to decrease the angle at a joint.

Extensor

Contracts to increase the angle at a joint.

05 — Cardiovascular System

Cardiac Muscle and Cardiac Output

Cardiac muscle

Cardiac muscle is specialised for continuous, rhythmic contraction and has a rich blood supply to support its high energy demand.

Myogenic contraction

The heart is myogenic: the contraction rhythm originates within the cardiac muscle itself.

Cardiac cycle

Atrial systole Atria contract
→
Ventricular systole Ventricles contract
→
Diastole Heart relaxes

Cardiac output

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume
Worked Example

A person has a heart rate of 75 beats min⁻¹ and a stroke volume of 80 cm³.

Cardiac output

= 75 × 80
= 6000 cm³ min⁻¹

= 6.0 dm³ min⁻¹

The role of valves

Valves prevent the backflow of blood and ensure that blood flows through the heart in the correct direction.

06 — Exercise Physiology

Exercise and Gas Exchange

During exercise

  • Muscle ATP demand increases.
  • Rate of respiration increases.
  • Oxygen demand increases.
  • Carbon dioxide production increases.
  • Heart rate increases.
  • Ventilation increases.

Respiratory minute ventilation

Minute ventilation = breathing rate × tidal volume
Worked Example

Breathing rate = 20 breaths min⁻¹
Tidal volume = 2.5 dm³

Minute ventilation

= 20 × 2.5
= 50 dm³ min⁻¹

Oxygen debt

After intense exercise, oxygen consumption can remain elevated while the body restores normal physiological conditions and processes accumulated metabolic products.

07 — Internal Environment

Homeostasis and Negative Feedback

Homeostasis: Maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite external or internal changes.

Negative feedback

Negative feedback reverses a change and returns a variable towards its set point or normal range.

Stimulus Variable changes
→
Receptor Detects change
→
Coordinator Processes information
→
Effector Produces response

Blood glucose regulation

High blood glucose

Pancreatic β cells release insulin. Cells increase glucose uptake and the liver converts glucose into glycogen.

Low blood glucose

Pancreatic α cells release glucagon. Glycogen is broken down and glucose is released into the blood.

Insulin → lowers blood glucose

Glucagon → raises blood glucose
08 — Excretion and Osmoregulation

Liver, Kidney and Internal Environment

Deamination

Excess amino acids cannot simply be stored. Their amino groups are removed in the liver.

Amino acid → ammonia → urea

Urea is transported in the blood to the kidneys for excretion.

Nephron structure

Glomerulus

Network of capillaries where ultrafiltration occurs.

Bowman's capsule

Collects the filtrate produced by ultrafiltration.

Loop of Henle

Important in creating the conditions needed for water reabsorption.

Ultrafiltration

High hydrostatic pressure forces small molecules from the blood through the filtration barrier into Bowman's capsule.

Large plasma proteins and blood cells normally remain in the blood because they are too large to pass through the filtration barrier.

Selective reabsorption

Useful substances such as glucose and required ions are reabsorbed from the filtrate.

ADH

Antidiuretic hormone increases the permeability of collecting ducts to water, allowing more water to be reabsorbed.

More ADH → greater water reabsorption → smaller volume of concentrated urine
09 — Topic 8

Nervous Coordination

Central and peripheral nervous systems

Central nervous system

Brain and spinal cord.

Peripheral nervous system

Nerves connecting the CNS with receptors and effectors.

Neurones

Sensory neurone

Carries impulses from receptors towards the CNS.

Relay neurone

Connects neurones within the CNS.

Motor neurone

Carries impulses from the CNS towards effectors.

Resting potential

A neurone at rest maintains a potential difference across its membrane through ion movements and selective permeability.

Resting potential ≈ −70 mV

Action potential

  1. A stimulus causes depolarisation if threshold is reached.
  2. Sodium ions enter the axon through voltage-gated channels.
  3. The membrane potential becomes positive.
  4. Sodium channels close and potassium channels open.
  5. Potassium ions leave the axon.
  6. The membrane repolarises.
All-or-nothing response: If threshold is reached, a full action potential occurs. Increasing stimulus strength does not increase the size of an individual action potential.
10 — Synapses

Synaptic Transmission

Sequence of transmission

  1. Action potential reaches the presynaptic membrane.
  2. Calcium ion channels open.
  3. Calcium ions enter the synaptic knob.
  4. Vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane.
  5. Neurotransmitter is released by exocytosis.
  6. Neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft.
  7. It binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
  8. Ion channels open and a response is generated.
Impulse Presynaptic neurone
→
Ca²⁺ Entry
→
Neurotransmitter Released
→
Receptor Postsynaptic response

Why are synapses important?

  • Ensure one-way transmission.
  • Allow integration of signals.
  • Permit excitation or inhibition.
  • Allow modification of nervous responses.

12 — Brain and Coordination

The Brain

Cerebral hemispheres

Responsible for conscious processing, voluntary movement and higher cognitive functions.

Hypothalamus

Important in homeostasis and control of the endocrine system.

Pituitary gland

Releases hormones involved in coordination of many physiological processes.

Cerebellum

Important in coordination and balance.

Medulla oblongata

Controls important involuntary processes.

Brain imaging

MRI

Produces detailed images of brain structures using magnetic fields and radio waves.

fMRI

Can identify changes in brain activity associated with changes in blood flow.

PET

Uses radioactive tracers to investigate metabolic activity.

CT

Uses X-rays to produce cross-sectional images of the body.

Brain-imaging methods provide different types of information. The technique chosen depends on the biological or medical question.
13 — Brain Chemistry

Neurotransmitters and Ill Health

Dopamine

Dopamine is an important neurotransmitter involved in several brain pathways. Changes in dopamine signalling are associated with conditions including Parkinson's disease.

Serotonin

Serotonin is involved in mood and other physiological processes. Altered serotonin signalling has been associated with depression.

Drug development:

Understanding neurotransmitter pathways can help scientists identify potential targets for therapeutic drugs.

DNA ligase

DNA Sequencing and Bioinformatics

DNA sequencing

DNA sequencing determines the order of bases in a DNA molecule.

Bioinformatics

Sequence comparison

16 — Plant Responses

Sensitivity and Responses in Plants

Auxin

Auxin is a plant hormone involved in growth responses.

Phototropism

Shoots generally grow towards light.

  1. Light is detected.
  2. Auxin distribution becomes uneven.
  3. Cells on the shaded side elongate more.
  4. The shoot bends towards the light.

Gravitropism

Plants also respond directionally to gravity. Roots and shoots show different growth responses.

Plant hormones can coordinate growth by changing cell elongation and development.

19 — Exam Practice

Unit 5 Practice

1. State the site of glycolysis.
Show answer

Cytoplasm.

2. Explain why oxygen is required during oxidative phosphorylation.
Show answer

Oxygen acts as the final electron acceptor at the end of the electron transport chain. It combines with electrons and hydrogen ions to form water.

3. Calculate cardiac output for a heart rate of 80 beats min⁻¹ and stroke volume of 75 cm³.
Show answer

Cardiac output = 80 × 75

= 6000 cm³ min⁻¹

= 6.0 dm³ min⁻¹

4. Explain how ATP contributes to muscle contraction.
Show answer

ATP provides energy for the cycling of myosin heads and is also required for processes such as calcium ion transport.

5. Explain the role of negative feedback in homeostasis.
Show answer

A change in an internal variable is detected, producing a response that opposes the original change and returns the variable towards its normal range.

6. Explain ultrafiltration in the kidney.
Show answer

High pressure in the glomerulus forces small molecules from the blood through the filtration barrier into Bowman's capsule. Blood cells and large plasma proteins remain in the blood.

7. Describe transmission across a synapse.
Show answer

An action potential causes calcium ions to enter the synaptic knob. Vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release neurotransmitter. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.

8. What is the role of DNA ligase in genetic engineering?
Show answer

DNA ligase joins DNA fragments by forming phosphodiester bonds in the sugar-phosphate backbone.

9. Describe the three stages of PCR.
Show answer

Denaturation separates the DNA strands. During annealing, primers bind to complementary sequences. During extension, DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to form new DNA strands.

20 — Exam Strategy

How to Approach Unit 5 Questions

1 — Build the biological sequence

For mechanisms, write the events in the correct order rather than listing disconnected facts.

2 — Link structure to function

For muscles, kidneys, neurones and eyes, explain why the structure allows the function.

3 — Use precise terminology

For example:

"protons move down an electrochemical gradient through ATP synthase"

is stronger than simply saying "protons make ATP."

4 — Show calculations

Write the equation, substitute values, calculate and give the correct unit.

5 — For gene technology, know the enzyme and its job

Restriction enzyme → cuts DNA
DNA ligase → joins DNA
DNA polymerase → synthesises DNA
Primers → provide starting points for DNA synthesis
