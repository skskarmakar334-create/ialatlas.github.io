Energetics, Group Chemistry, Halogenoalkanes and Alcohols

TOPIC 01

Energetics

Energetics is the study of energy changes that occur during chemical reactions.

Enthalpy Change

The enthalpy change of a reaction is the heat energy change when reactants are converted into products under specified conditions.

ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)

Exothermic Reactions

An exothermic reaction releases energy to the surroundings. The products have lower enthalpy than the reactants.

ΔH < 0

Endothermic Reactions

An endothermic reaction absorbs energy from the surroundings. The products have higher enthalpy than the reactants.

ΔH > 0

Calorimetry

Calorimetry can be used to determine the energy transferred during a reaction.

q = mcΔT
  • q = energy transferred
  • m = mass of solution
  • c = specific heat capacity
  • ΔT = temperature change

Worked Example

A reaction heats 100 g of water by 15 °C. Take the specific heat capacity of water as 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

q = 100 × 4.18 × 15 = 6270 J

Therefore, 6.27 kJ of energy is transferred to the water.

Bond Enthalpy

Bond breaking requires energy, while bond formation releases energy.

ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)

Hess's Law

Hess's Law states that the total enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken between reactants and products.

TOPIC 02

Intermolecular Forces

Intermolecular forces act between molecules and influence physical properties such as boiling point and melting point.

London Forces

London forces arise from temporary fluctuations in electron density and occur between all atoms and molecules.

Larger molecules generally have stronger London forces because they contain more electrons and have more easily polarised electron clouds.

Permanent Dipole-Dipole Forces

These occur between polar molecules because of attractions between permanent partial charges.

Hydrogen Bonding

Hydrogen bonding is a particularly strong intermolecular force involving hydrogen bonded to a highly electronegative atom, such as nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine.

Key idea:

Stronger intermolecular forces generally require more energy to overcome, leading to higher boiling temperatures.

TOPIC 03

Redox Chemistry

Redox reactions involve the transfer of electrons.

Oxidation and Reduction

Remember:

Oxidation is loss of electrons. Reduction is gain of electrons.

OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain

Oxidation Numbers

Oxidation numbers can be used to identify oxidation and reduction during chemical reactions.

  • Oxidation number increases → oxidation.
  • Oxidation number decreases → reduction.

Half Equations

Half equations show the electron transfer occurring during oxidation or reduction.

Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

TOPIC 04

Group 1 — The Alkali Metals

Group 1 contains lithium, sodium, potassium and other alkali metals.

Reactivity

Reactivity increases down the group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and is more easily removed.

Reaction with Water

Group 1 metals react with water to produce an alkaline solution and hydrogen gas.

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂

Reaction with Oxygen

Group 1 metals react with oxygen to form oxides and, for some heavier members, other oxygen-containing compounds.

TOPIC 05

Group 2 — The Alkaline Earth Metals

Group 2 contains beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium and radium.

Reactivity

Reactivity generally increases down the group because the outer electrons become easier to remove.

Reaction with Water

The reactions become more vigorous down the group.

Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂

Solubility of Hydroxides

The solubility of Group 2 hydroxides generally increases down the group.

Solubility of Sulfates

The solubility of Group 2 sulfates generally decreases down the group.

TOPIC 06

Group 7 — The Halogens

Group 7 elements are reactive non-metals that exist as diatomic molecules.

F₂ • Cl₂ • Br₂ • I₂

Reactivity

Reactivity decreases down the group because the outer shell is further from the nucleus and attraction for an incoming electron becomes weaker.

Displacement Reactions

A more reactive halogen can displace a less reactive halide ion from solution.

Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂

Halide Ion Tests

Aqueous silver nitrate can be used to distinguish chloride, bromide and iodide ions.

  • Chloride → white precipitate.
  • Bromide → cream precipitate.
  • Iodide → yellow precipitate.

TOPIC 07

Introduction to Kinetics

Kinetics concerns the rate at which chemical reactions occur.

Collision Theory

For a reaction to occur, particles must collide with sufficient energy and with the appropriate orientation.

Activation Energy

Activation energy is the minimum energy required for a successful reaction.

Important:

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy.

Factors Affecting Rate

  • Temperature
  • Concentration
  • Pressure for gaseous reactions
  • Surface area of a solid
  • Presence of a catalyst

Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution

Increasing temperature gives particles a greater average kinetic energy and increases the proportion of particles with energy greater than the activation energy.

TOPIC 08

Introduction to Equilibria

A reversible reaction can reach a state of dynamic equilibrium.

Forward rate = Reverse rate

At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant, although both forward and reverse reactions continue.

Le Chatelier's Principle

When a system at equilibrium is disturbed, the equilibrium position shifts in the direction that opposes the change.

Temperature

Increasing temperature favours the endothermic direction. Decreasing temperature favours the exothermic direction.

Pressure

For gaseous equilibria, increasing pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas.

Catalysts

A catalyst speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally. It does not change the equilibrium position.

TOPIC 09

Alcohols

Alcohols contain the hydroxyl functional group, −OH.

General functional group: R−OH

Classification

  • Primary alcohols
  • Secondary alcohols
  • Tertiary alcohols

Combustion

Alcohols can undergo complete combustion to form carbon dioxide and water.

CH₃CH₂OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Oxidation

Primary alcohols can be oxidised first to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids.

Primary alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid

Secondary alcohols can be oxidised to ketones. Tertiary alcohols are resistant to oxidation under typical laboratory conditions.

Dehydration

Alcohols can undergo dehydration to form alkenes.

Alcohol → Alkene + H₂O

TOPIC 10

Halogenoalkanes

Halogenoalkanes contain a halogen atom bonded to a carbon atom.

R−X

where X may be F, Cl, Br or I.

Nucleophilic Substitution

Halogenoalkanes can react with nucleophiles in substitution reactions.

Reaction with Aqueous Hydroxide

Aqueous hydroxide ions can replace the halogen atom to produce an alcohol.

R−Br + OH⁻ → R−OH + Br⁻

Reaction with Cyanide

Cyanide ions can replace the halogen and increase the carbon chain by one carbon atom.

R−Br + CN⁻ → R−CN + Br⁻

Reaction with Ammonia

Halogenoalkanes can react with ammonia to form amines.

Hydrolysis

The rate of hydrolysis depends on the carbon-halogen bond. Iodoalkanes generally hydrolyse more readily than bromoalkanes, which hydrolyse more readily than chloroalkanes.

TOPIC 11

Organic Spectra

Spectroscopic techniques provide information that can be used to identify organic compounds.

Infrared Spectroscopy

Infrared spectroscopy can be used to identify functional groups from characteristic absorption regions.

  • O−H absorption → broad absorption at higher wavenumbers.
  • C=O absorption → strong absorption around 1700 cm⁻¹.
  • C−H bonds → absorptions in the appropriate C−H region.

Mass Spectrometry

Mass spectrometry can provide information about molecular mass and fragmentation.

Molecular ion:

The molecular ion peak can provide the relative molecular mass of an organic compound.

Using Spectra Together

Different analytical techniques can be combined to identify an unknown organic compound.

Unit 2 Mixed Practice

1. A reaction transfers 8.36 kJ of energy to 200 g of water. Calculate the temperature rise. Take c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Show answer

q = mcΔT

8360 = 200 × 4.18 × ΔT

ΔT = 10.0 K

2. Explain why boiling temperatures generally increase as the size of molecules increases.
Show answer

Larger molecules generally have more electrons and are more polarizable, producing stronger London forces. More energy is therefore required to separate the molecules.

3. Explain why the reactivity of Group 1 metals increases down the group.
Show answer

The outer electron is further from the nucleus and is increasingly shielded by inner electron shells. The attraction between the nucleus and outer electron decreases, so the electron is lost more easily.

4. Chlorine is added to potassium bromide solution. Write the ionic equation.
Show answer

Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂

5. Explain why increasing temperature increases reaction rate.
Show answer

Particles have greater average kinetic energy. Collisions occur more frequently and a greater proportion of particles have energy greater than the activation energy.

6. State what happens to an equilibrium when pressure is increased in a gaseous reaction.
Show answer

The equilibrium shifts towards the side with fewer moles of gas, provided the two sides contain different numbers of gaseous moles.

7. Give the oxidation products of a primary alcohol.
Show answer

A primary alcohol can be oxidised to an aldehyde and then further oxidised to a carboxylic acid.

8. Write the reaction of bromoethane with aqueous hydroxide ions.
Show answer

CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻

9. Which halide ion gives a cream precipitate with aqueous silver nitrate?
Show answer

Bromide ions, Br⁻.

10. State the role of a catalyst in an equilibrium reaction.
Show answer

A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy. It speeds up both the forward and reverse reactions and does not change the equilibrium position.
