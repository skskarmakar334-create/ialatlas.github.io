TOPIC 01

Water and Biological Molecules

Biological molecules form the chemical basis of living organisms. Unit 1 begins with water, carbohydrates and lipids, linking their structures to their biological functions.

Water

Water is essential for life and makes up a large proportion of living organisms. Its properties arise from the polar nature of the water molecule.

Water is a polar molecule.

The oxygen atom is more electronegative than the hydrogen atoms, so oxygen carries a partial negative charge while the hydrogen atoms carry partial positive charges.

Water as a Solvent

Water is an effective solvent for many ionic and polar substances. This makes it important in transport and metabolic reactions.

  • Mineral ions can dissolve in water.
  • Glucose and other polar molecules can be transported in aqueous solutions.
  • Many metabolic reactions occur in aqueous environments.
  • Blood plasma is largely water and acts as a transport medium.

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. They include monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides.

Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides are single sugar units. Examples include glucose, fructose and galactose.

Disaccharides

Disaccharides consist of two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond.

Condensation reaction

A condensation reaction joins molecules together while producing a molecule of water.

Hydrolysis reaction

Hydrolysis breaks a bond using water.

Polysaccharides

Polysaccharides are large carbohydrate molecules made from many monosaccharide units.

Polysaccharide Main role Important feature
Starch Energy storage in plants Compact and insoluble
Glycogen Energy storage in animals and fungi Highly branched
Cellulose Structural support in plant cell walls Long chains forming strong fibres

Starch

Starch contains two types of glucose polymer: amylose and amylopectin.

  • Amylose is an unbranched chain of α-glucose.
  • Amylopectin is branched.

Glycogen

Glycogen is the main storage polysaccharide in animals. Its extensive branching allows glucose units to be released rapidly when required.

Lipids

Triglycerides are formed from one glycerol molecule and three fatty acid molecules.

Glycerol + 3 fatty acids → triglyceride + 3H2O

The bonds formed between glycerol and fatty acids are ester bonds.

Saturated and Unsaturated Fatty Acids

  • Saturated fatty acids contain no carbon-carbon double bonds.
  • Unsaturated fatty acids contain one or more carbon-carbon double bonds.
Biological importance of triglycerides

Triglycerides provide a concentrated energy store and can also provide insulation and protection around organs.

TOPIC 02

The Cardiovascular System and Transport

Large multicellular organisms require mass transport systems because diffusion alone cannot supply all cells efficiently.

Why Mass Transport Is Necessary

As organisms become larger, their surface area to volume ratio decreases. Internal cells become farther from exchange surfaces, increasing the distance over which substances would need to diffuse.

Mass transport

The bulk movement of substances through an organism, usually using a transport system such as the circulatory system.

Blood Vessels

Blood vessel Structure Function
Artery Thick muscular and elastic wall; small lumen Carries blood away from the heart at high pressure
Vein Thinner wall; large lumen; valves Returns blood to the heart at lower pressure
Capillary Very narrow lumen and wall one cell thick Exchange of substances with tissues

The Mammalian Heart

The mammalian heart is a muscular organ containing four chambers: the right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium and left ventricle.

Major Blood Vessels

  • Vena cava
  • Pulmonary artery
  • Pulmonary vein
  • Aorta

Heart Valves

Valves prevent the backflow of blood and help maintain one-directional movement through the heart.

Cardiac Cycle

The cardiac cycle consists of alternating periods of atrial and ventricular contraction and relaxation.

Key sequence:

Atria contract → ventricles contract → heart muscle relaxes.

The valves open and close according to differences in pressure.

Double Circulation

Mammals have a double circulatory system. Blood passes through the heart twice during one complete circuit.

  1. The pulmonary circulation carries deoxygenated blood from the heart to the lungs and oxygenated blood back to the heart.
  2. The systemic circulation carries oxygenated blood from the heart to the body and returns deoxygenated blood to the heart.

Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin is a globular protein found inside red blood cells. It transports oxygen from the lungs to tissues.

Oxygen Dissociation Curves

The oxygen dissociation curve shows the relationship between oxygen concentration or partial pressure and the percentage saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen.

Haemoglobin + O2 ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin

The Bohr Effect

An increase in carbon dioxide concentration causes haemoglobin to have a lower affinity for oxygen. This shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the right.

Why this is useful:

Active tissues produce more carbon dioxide. The Bohr effect therefore encourages haemoglobin to release more oxygen where respiration is occurring rapidly.

Fetal Haemoglobin

Fetal haemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen than adult haemoglobin. This allows the fetus to obtain oxygen from the mother's blood across the placenta.

TOPIC 03

Cardiovascular Disease, Risk and Health

Cardiovascular disease involves conditions affecting the heart and blood vessels. Unit 1 examines atherosclerosis, blood clotting, risk factors, evidence and treatments.

Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis involves changes to artery walls and the formation of plaques that can restrict blood flow.

Development of Atherosclerosis

  1. Damage or dysfunction occurs in the endothelium.
  2. An inflammatory response develops.
  3. White blood cells and other substances accumulate.
  4. A plaque develops within the artery wall.
  5. The artery becomes narrower and blood flow can be restricted.
Important:

Atherosclerosis can increase blood pressure because blood has to flow through a narrower vessel.

Blood Clotting

Blood clotting reduces blood loss after damage to a blood vessel. It also contributes to cardiovascular disease when inappropriate clots block blood vessels.

Prothrombin → Thrombin
Fibrinogen → Fibrin

Thrombin catalyses the conversion of fibrinogen into fibrin. Fibrin forms a mesh that helps trap blood cells and contributes to clot formation.

Risk Factors for Cardiovascular Disease

  • Genetic factors
  • Diet
  • Age
  • Gender
  • High blood pressure
  • Smoking
  • Physical inactivity

Dietary Antioxidants

Antioxidants can react with reactive molecules and may reduce oxidative damage. Their possible relationship with cardiovascular disease must be considered using evidence rather than assumption.

Correlation and Causation

Correlation does not automatically prove causation.

Two variables may change together because one causes the other, because both are influenced by another variable, or simply because of chance.

Evaluating Health Studies

A biological study should be evaluated by considering its sample, methods, variables, reliability, validity and the strength of the evidence.

  • Sample size
  • Representative sampling
  • Control of variables
  • Reliable measurements
  • Repeatability
  • Statistical analysis
  • Possible confounding variables

Perceived and Actual Risk

People do not always perceive health risks accurately. Media coverage, personal experience and the way information is presented can influence perception of risk.

Cholesterol

Cholesterol is transported through the blood in lipoproteins. High-density lipoprotein and low-density lipoprotein have different roles in cholesterol transport.

Lipoprotein General role
LDL Transports cholesterol from the liver towards tissues
HDL Transports cholesterol towards the liver

Reducing Cardiovascular Risk

  • Balanced diet
  • Appropriate physical activity
  • Not smoking
  • Managing high blood pressure
  • Managing cholesterol levels
  • Maintaining a healthy body composition

Treatments

Different treatments target different cardiovascular risk factors and processes.

  • Antihypertensives reduce high blood pressure.
  • Statins reduce blood cholesterol levels.
  • Anticoagulants reduce blood clot formation.
  • Platelet inhibitors reduce platelet aggregation.
Exam evaluation:

Treatments should be evaluated by considering both their benefits and possible risks or side effects.

TOPIC 04

Cell Membranes and Transport

Cell membranes control the movement of substances into and out of cells. Their structure explains their selective permeability.

Fluid Mosaic Model

The cell-surface membrane is described using the fluid mosaic model.

  • Phospholipid bilayer
  • Integral proteins
  • Peripheral proteins
  • Cholesterol
  • Glycoproteins
Phospholipid bilayer

Hydrophilic phosphate heads face the aqueous environments while hydrophobic fatty acid tails face inward, away from water.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

Facilitated Diffusion

Facilitated diffusion is passive movement through membrane proteins. Channel and carrier proteins can allow particular substances to cross the membrane.

Active Transport

Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient and requires energy from ATP.

ATP → ADP + Pi + energy

Endocytosis

Endocytosis involves the cell membrane surrounding material and forming a vesicle to bring it into the cell.

Exocytosis

Exocytosis involves vesicles fusing with the cell membrane and releasing their contents outside the cell.

Factors Affecting Diffusion

  • Concentration difference
  • Surface area
  • Diffusion distance
  • Temperature
  • Properties of the substance

Fick's Law

Rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × concentration difference) / diffusion distance

A large surface area, large concentration difference and short diffusion distance increase the rate of diffusion.

Osmosis

Osmosis is the net movement of free water molecules across a partially permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.

Water potential

Pure water has the highest water potential. Dissolving solutes in water lowers its water potential.

Water Potential

The movement of water can be explained using water potential rather than simply describing water moving from a dilute solution to a concentrated solution.

Water moves from higher Ψ to lower Ψ

TOPIC 05

Proteins and Enzymes

Proteins have a wide range of biological roles. Their function depends on their three-dimensional structure.

Amino Acids

Amino acids contain an amino group, a carboxyl group and a variable R group.

NH2−CHR−COOH

Peptide Bonds

A condensation reaction between amino acids forms a peptide bond. A polypeptide is a chain of amino acids joined by peptide bonds.

Levels of Protein Structure

  • Primary: amino acid sequence.
  • Secondary: local folding such as α-helices and β-pleated sheets.
  • Tertiary: overall three-dimensional structure of one polypeptide.
  • Quaternary: association of multiple polypeptide chains.

Protein Bonds and Interactions

  • Hydrogen bonds
  • Ionic interactions
  • Disulfide bonds
  • Hydrophobic interactions

Globular and Fibrous Proteins

Globular proteins tend to have compact structures and include many enzymes and haemoglobin. Fibrous proteins tend to have longer structures and often have structural roles.

Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin is a globular protein adapted for oxygen transport. It contains four polypeptide chains and haem groups containing iron.

Collagen

Collagen is a fibrous protein that provides structural strength in connective tissues.

Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They increase reaction rates by lowering the activation energy without being permanently changed by the reaction.

Activation energy

The minimum energy required for a reaction to occur.

Enzyme Specificity

The active site of an enzyme has a particular shape and chemical environment. Only suitable substrates can bind effectively.

Induced Fit

When a substrate binds, interactions between the substrate and enzyme can cause the active site to change shape slightly. This can help position the substrate correctly for reaction.

Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

  • Temperature
  • pH
  • Substrate concentration
  • Enzyme concentration

Temperature

Increasing temperature generally increases kinetic energy and collision frequency. Above the optimum temperature, bonds maintaining enzyme structure can be disrupted and the enzyme may become denatured.

pH

Extreme pH can alter ionic interactions and hydrogen bonding within a protein, changing the shape of the active site.

Initial Rate

The initial rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction is determined from the steepest early part of a reaction progress graph.

Initial rate = change in product / change in time

TOPIC 06

DNA, RNA and Genetic Information

DNA stores genetic information. RNA molecules help convert this information into proteins.

Nucleotides

A nucleotide contains a pentose sugar, phosphate group and nitrogen-containing base.

DNA Bases

  • Adenine
  • Thymine
  • Cytosine
  • Guanine
Complementary base pairing

Adenine pairs with thymine, while cytosine pairs with guanine. Hydrogen bonds hold complementary bases together.

DNA Structure

DNA consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands forming a double helix.

Nucleotides within each strand are joined by phosphodiester bonds.

RNA

RNA is generally single-stranded and contains ribose sugar. Uracil replaces thymine.

DNA Replication

Before cell division, DNA must be copied. DNA replication is semi-conservative.

  1. The DNA double helix unwinds.
  2. Hydrogen bonds between complementary bases break.
  3. Each original strand acts as a template.
  4. DNA polymerase joins complementary nucleotides.
  5. Two DNA molecules are produced, each containing one original strand and one new strand.

Meselson-Stahl Evidence

The Meselson-Stahl experiment provided evidence supporting semi-conservative DNA replication using different nitrogen isotopes and density measurements.

The Genetic Code

The genetic code is a triplet code. Three bases form a codon that specifies an amino acid or a termination signal.

  • Triplet
  • Non-overlapping
  • Degenerate
Degenerate code

More than one codon can code for the same amino acid.

Genes

A gene is a sequence of DNA bases that codes for a polypeptide or functional RNA.

TOPIC 07

Protein Synthesis, Mutations and Inheritance

Genetic information is expressed through transcription and translation. Mutations and inheritance help explain variation within populations.

Transcription

During transcription, RNA polymerase uses one DNA strand as a template to produce a complementary RNA molecule.

Translation

During translation, ribosomes use the sequence of codons on mRNA to assemble a polypeptide.

  • mRNA carries the genetic message.
  • tRNA carries amino acids.
  • Anticodons on tRNA pair with complementary mRNA codons.
  • The ribosome forms peptide bonds between amino acids.

Start and Stop Codons

Translation begins at a start codon and ends when a stop codon is reached.

Mutations

A mutation is a change in the DNA base sequence.

Substitution

One base is replaced by another.

Insertion

One or more bases are added to a sequence.

Deletion

One or more bases are removed.

Mutation effects:

A mutation may have no observable effect, may alter a protein's function, or may contribute to disease. The effect depends on where and how the DNA sequence changes.

Genetic Terminology

Term Meaning
Gene A sequence of DNA associated with a characteristic or functional product
Allele An alternative form of a gene
Genotype The alleles an individual possesses
Phenotype Observable characteristics resulting from genotype and environment
Homozygous Two identical alleles
Heterozygous Two different alleles
Dominant An allele expressed in a heterozygote
Recessive An allele expressed when no dominant allele masks it
Codominant Both alleles contribute to the phenotype

Monohybrid Inheritance

A genetic cross can be represented using genetic diagrams. The expected proportions of offspring depend on the parental genotypes.

WORKED GENETIC EXAMPLE

Suppose two heterozygous individuals have genotypes Aa × Aa.

Possible offspring genotypes:

AA, Aa, Aa, aa

Genotypic ratio: 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa

Phenotypic proportions depend on whether A is dominant and on the characteristic being studied.

Sex Linkage

Some genes are located on the sex chromosomes. X-linked conditions can show different inheritance patterns in males and females.

Cystic Fibrosis

Cystic fibrosis is a genetic disorder associated with mutations in the CFTR gene. Changes in the CFTR protein affect chloride ion transport and can alter the properties of mucus.

The disorder can affect the respiratory, digestive and reproductive systems.

Genetic Screening

Genetic screening can identify individuals who carry particular alleles or determine whether a fetus or embryo may have a genetic condition.

  • Carrier screening
  • Preimplantation genetic diagnosis
  • Amniocentesis
  • Chorionic villus sampling

Ethical and Social Issues

Genetic screening raises scientific, ethical and social questions. Different viewpoints should be considered when evaluating issues such as privacy, reproductive choice, possible discrimination, accuracy and psychological impact.

Biology Unit 1 Practical Skills

Practical work is an important part of Edexcel IAL Biology. Unit 1 includes four core practical areas.

CORE PRACTICAL 1 — FOOD TESTS

Reducing Sugars

Benedict's reagent can be used to test for reducing sugars. The sample is heated with Benedict's reagent and the resulting colour is compared with suitable standards.

A positive result produces a colour change from the original blue reagent towards green, yellow, orange or red depending on concentration.

Starch

Iodine solution is used to test for starch. A positive result produces a blue-black colour.

CORE PRACTICAL 2 — VITAMIN C

The vitamin C content of foods or drinks can be compared using a suitable quantitative method.

Exam Considerations

  • Control variables
  • Repeat measurements
  • Use of standards
  • Accurate volume measurements
  • Reliability
  • Validity

The effect of factors such as temperature and alcohol concentration on membrane permeability can be investigated.

Changes in membrane permeability can be measured using an appropriate indicator or quantitative measurement.

Conditions that disrupt membrane structure can increase the movement of substances across the membrane.

PRACTICE

Unit 1 Mixed Practice

Try each question before opening the answer. These questions cover molecules, transport, health, membranes, proteins, DNA and genetics.

1. Explain why water is an effective transport medium in organisms.
Show answer

Water is polar and can dissolve many ionic and polar substances. Therefore substances such as mineral ions and glucose can be transported in aqueous solutions.

2. Explain why glycogen is suitable for energy storage in animals.
Show answer

Glycogen is compact and highly branched. Its branching provides many ends from which glucose units can be hydrolysed rapidly when energy is required.

3. Explain why mammals require a mass transport system.
Show answer

Mammals are large multicellular organisms with a relatively small surface area to volume ratio. Many cells are far from exchange surfaces, so diffusion alone would be too slow to supply their metabolic demands.

4. Compare the structure of arteries and veins.
Show answer

Arteries have thick muscular and elastic walls and relatively small lumens because they carry blood at high pressure. Veins have thinner walls, larger lumens and valves because blood returns at lower pressure.

5. Explain the Bohr effect.
Show answer

Increasing carbon dioxide concentration decreases the affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen. This causes the oxygen dissociation curve to shift to the right, promoting oxygen unloading in actively respiring tissues.

6. Explain how atherosclerosis can increase cardiovascular risk.
Show answer

Plaques can narrow arteries and reduce blood flow. They can also increase the risk of blood clots blocking vessels. Narrower arteries can contribute to increased blood pressure.

7. State the direction of net water movement during osmosis.
Show answer

Water moves from higher water potential to lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane.

8. Explain why active transport requires ATP.
Show answer

Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient. Energy from ATP is used by membrane proteins to drive this movement.

9. Explain why an enzyme's activity decreases above its optimum temperature.
Show answer

High temperatures disrupt bonds maintaining the enzyme's three-dimensional structure. The active site changes shape, so fewer substrate molecules bind effectively.

10. What is meant by semi-conservative DNA replication?
Show answer

Each newly formed DNA molecule contains one original strand and one newly synthesised strand.

11. Distinguish between a gene and an allele.
Show answer

A gene is a DNA sequence associated with a particular functional product or characteristic. An allele is an alternative form of a gene.

12. Explain why insertion or deletion mutations can have large effects on a protein.
Show answer

An insertion or deletion can alter the reading frame of the genetic code. This can change many subsequent codons and therefore the amino acid sequence of the polypeptide.
