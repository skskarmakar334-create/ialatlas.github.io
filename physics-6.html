UNIT OVERVIEW

Practical Skills in Physics II

Unit 6 assesses practical knowledge and understanding developed while studying Units 4 and 5. The focus is not simply on carrying out an experiment, but on understanding how experiments are designed, measured, analysed and evaluated.

Core idea

A good experimental investigation connects a clear aim, suitable variables, reliable measurements, appropriate analysis and a justified conclusion.

Key areas

  • Planning experiments
  • Experimental techniques
  • Measurements and units
  • Uncertainties and errors
  • Data processing
  • Graphs and gradients
  • Logarithmic graphs
  • Identifying relationships
  • Evaluation and improvements
  • Drawing conclusions

TOPIC 01

Planning an Experiment

A successful investigation begins with a clear experimental plan. The plan should allow the relationship between relevant variables to be investigated quantitatively.

Experimental Aim

The aim should clearly state what relationship or physical quantity is being investigated.

Example

Investigate how the period of a pendulum depends on its length.

Independent Variable

The independent variable is the quantity deliberately changed by the experimenter.

Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is the quantity measured in response to the independent variable.

Control Variables

Control variables are quantities that should be kept constant so that they do not affect the relationship being investigated.

Exam tip

When planning, always identify what you change, what you measure and what must remain constant.

TOPIC 02

Variables and Experimental Design

The design of an experiment should allow the effect of one variable on another to be investigated fairly.

Range of Values

The independent variable should normally be varied over a sufficiently large range to make the relationship clear.

Repeated Measurements

Repeating measurements can help identify random variation and improve confidence in the measured value.

Mean Measurements

If repeated measurements are appropriate, a mean can be used to represent the measurements.

Mean = Sum of measurements / Number of measurements

Anomalous Results

An anomalous result is a measurement that does not fit the general pattern of the other results.

Anomalies should not automatically be removed. There should be a justified reason for treating a result as anomalous.

TOPIC 03

Measurements and Units

Accurate recording of physical quantities is essential in experimental physics.

SI Units

  • Length — metre (m)
  • Mass — kilogram (kg)
  • Time — second (s)
  • Current — ampere (A)
  • Temperature — kelvin (K)

Prefixes

Experimental measurements may involve a wide range of scales. Values should be converted consistently when required.

  • giga = 10⁹
  • mega = 10⁶
  • kilo = 10³
  • centi = 10⁻²
  • milli = 10⁻³
  • micro = 10⁻⁶
  • nano = 10⁻⁹
Remember

Always include units in tables, calculations and graphs where appropriate.

TOPIC 04

Uncertainties

Every experimental measurement has some uncertainty. Understanding uncertainty is essential when evaluating experimental results.

Absolute Uncertainty

Absolute uncertainty expresses the uncertainty in the same units as the measured quantity.

Example

A length is measured as 20.0 cm with an uncertainty of ±0.1 cm.

Percentage Uncertainty

Percentage uncertainty = (Absolute uncertainty / measured value) × 100%

Compound Percentage Uncertainty

When quantities are multiplied, divided or raised to powers, percentage uncertainties can be combined according to the mathematical relationship.

Example

If a quantity is calculated from two measured quantities that are multiplied together, the percentage uncertainties are added.

TOPIC 05

Random and Systematic Errors

Random Errors

Random errors cause measurements to vary unpredictably around a value.

Repeating measurements and calculating a mean can reduce the effect of random variation.

Systematic Errors

Systematic errors produce a consistent shift in measurements.

Repeating the measurement does not necessarily remove a systematic error.

Important distinction

Random errors mainly affect precision, while systematic errors can affect the accuracy of measurements.

TOPIC 06

Accuracy, Precision and Sensitivity

Accuracy

Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the accepted or true value.

Precision

Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity describes the ability of an instrument or method to detect small changes in the quantity being measured.

Exam language

Do not use accuracy and precision as interchangeable terms. They describe different aspects of measurement quality.

TOPIC 07

Experimental Techniques

Unit 6 can draw on practical techniques developed while studying the experimental content of Units 4 and 5.

General Good Practice

  • Use suitable measuring instruments.
  • Check equipment before taking measurements.
  • Record measurements consistently.
  • Use an appropriate range of values.
  • Repeat measurements where appropriate.
  • Control relevant variables.
  • Record observations clearly.
  • Use appropriate safety precautions.

Improving Measurements

Measurements can often be improved by choosing an appropriate instrument, increasing the scale of the measurement, repeating readings or using a more suitable experimental arrangement.

TOPIC 08

Recording Experimental Data

Experimental results should be recorded in a clear table before detailed analysis is performed.

Good Data Tables

  • Give each quantity a clear heading.
  • Include units in the headings.
  • Use consistent decimal places where appropriate.
  • Keep measured and calculated values clearly identifiable.
  • Record enough significant figures for the experiment.

Example table structure

Length / m      Time / s

0.20          0.91

0.30          1.10

0.40          1.27

TOPIC 09

Drawing and Using Graphs

Graphs are one of the most important tools for analysing experimental relationships.

Choosing Axes

The independent variable is normally placed on the horizontal axis and the dependent variable on the vertical axis.

Graph Requirements

  • Label both axes.
  • Include units.
  • Use an appropriate scale.
  • Use the available graph space effectively.
  • Plot points accurately.
  • Draw a suitable line or curve of best fit.

Gradient

The gradient of a graph can be used to determine a physical constant or establish a mathematical relationship.

Gradient = Change in y / Change in x
Large triangle method

When finding a gradient from a best-fit line, use a large triangle rather than a tiny triangle to reduce the effect of drawing and reading uncertainty.

TOPIC 10

Logarithmic Graphs

Log graphs can be used to investigate relationships that are not directly linear.

Power Relationships

Suppose two variables follow a relationship of the form:

y = axⁿ

Taking logarithms gives:

log(y) = log(a) + n log(x)

Therefore, plotting log(y) against log(x) produces a straight line when the power relationship is valid.

Finding the Power

The gradient of the log-log graph gives the power n.

Gradient = n

Finding the Constant

The intercept can be used to determine the constant a.

Intercept = log(a)
Key idea

Unit 6 expects students to be comfortable using graphs to determine relationships and constants, including logarithmic graphs.

TOPIC 11

Determining Relationships

Experimental data can be used to determine how one physical quantity depends on another.

Linear Relationship

y = mx + c

The gradient gives m and the vertical intercept gives c.

Direct Proportionality

y ∝ x

A graph through the origin supports a direct proportional relationship.

Power Relationship

y ∝ xⁿ

A log-log graph can be used to determine the value of n.

TOPIC 12

Data Analysis

Experimental analysis involves converting raw measurements into useful physical information.

Calculations

Calculations should use appropriate equations and maintain consistent units.

Significant Figures

Final answers should normally be given to an appropriate number of significant figures based on the precision of the measurements.

Patterns and Trends

Experimental data should be examined for patterns such as proportionality, linearity, curves, anomalies and changes in gradient.

Example

If doubling x approximately doubles y, the results may provide evidence for a direct proportional relationship.

TOPIC 13

Evaluating an Experiment

Evaluation requires more than saying that an experiment was "good" or "bad". The evaluation should be connected to evidence from the experiment.

Sources of Error

Identify realistic sources of uncertainty or error that could affect the measurements.

Limitations

Consider limitations of the apparatus, method, measurement range and experimental design.

Improvements

Improvements should directly address an identified limitation.

Weak evaluation

"Use better equipment."

Better evaluation

"Use a digital timer with a smaller measurement uncertainty to reduce uncertainty in the measured period."

TOPIC 14

Drawing Conclusions

A conclusion should answer the original experimental aim using evidence obtained from the data.

A Strong Conclusion

  • States the relationship found.
  • Uses quantitative evidence where appropriate.
  • Refers to the graph or calculated value.
  • Addresses the original aim.
  • Acknowledges uncertainty where relevant.
Remember

A conclusion should be based on the experimental evidence, not simply on what theory predicts.

TOPIC 15

Comparing Experimental and Accepted Values

Experimental results can be compared with accepted or theoretical values.

Percentage Difference

Percentage difference = |Experimental − Accepted| / Accepted × 100%

A small percentage difference may indicate good agreement, but the size of experimental uncertainty must also be considered.

Exam tip

Do not claim that an experimental result is wrong merely because it differs slightly from an accepted value. Consider uncertainty.

PRACTICE MODE

Unit 6 Mixed Practice

1. State the difference between an independent variable and a dependent variable.
Show answer

The independent variable is deliberately changed, while the dependent variable is measured in response.

2. Explain why repeated measurements can be useful.
Show answer

Repeated measurements can reveal random variation, allow a mean to be calculated and increase confidence in the measured value.

3. A length is measured as 50.0 cm ± 0.5 cm. Calculate the percentage uncertainty.
Show answer

Percentage uncertainty = (0.5 / 50.0) × 100 = 1.0%.

4. Explain the difference between accuracy and precision.
Show answer

Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the accepted value. Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with each other.

5. Why is a large triangle normally used when calculating the gradient of a best-fit line?
Show answer

A large triangle reduces the relative effect of small uncertainties in reading the coordinates and therefore gives a more reliable gradient.

6. A relationship is believed to have the form y = axⁿ. Which graph can be used to determine n?
Show answer

Plot log(y) against log(x). The gradient of the straight-line graph gives n.

7. State two features of a well-designed experimental table.
Show answer

Quantities should have clear headings with appropriate units, and measurements should be recorded consistently.

8. Give one way to reduce the effect of random error.
Show answer

Repeat measurements and use an appropriate mean.

9. Explain why repeating a measurement does not necessarily remove systematic error.
Show answer

A systematic error produces a consistent shift in the measurements, so repeating the same flawed measurement does not remove the underlying bias.

10. What should a good experimental conclusion contain?
Show answer

It should answer the original aim and use quantitative evidence from the experiment, while considering uncertainty where appropriate.

11. What is the purpose of a control variable?
Show answer

A control variable is kept constant so that it does not affect the relationship between the independent and dependent variables.

12. What does the gradient of a log(y) against log(x) graph represent for y = axⁿ?
Show answer

The gradient represents the exponent n.

13. Why should anomalous results not automatically be removed?
Show answer

An apparent anomaly may contain useful experimental information. There should be a justified reason for excluding a result rather than removing it simply because it does not fit the expected pattern.

14. State one difference between random and systematic error.
Show answer

Random errors cause unpredictable variation between measurements, whereas systematic errors tend to produce a consistent bias or shift.

15. Why should experimental uncertainty be considered when comparing an experimental value with an accepted value?
Show answer

A difference between the experimental and accepted values may be consistent with the experimental uncertainty. Therefore, the uncertainty must be considered before deciding whether the results agree.
